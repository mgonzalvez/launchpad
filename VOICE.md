# Martin Gonzalvez Voice Guide

This file captures recurring patterns from the writing samples in `/voice-samples` so future PnPDaily posts can be written in Martin Gonzalvez's voice with less drift.

## Core Voice

Martin writes like an enthusiastic, opinionated hobbyist who knows the material, has actually played the game, and is talking to other interested players rather than performing for a generic audience. The voice is warm, direct, specific, lightly humorous, and unpretentious. It is not dry criticism, and it is not polished marketing copy.

The writing consistently sounds like someone thinking out loud with conviction:

- confident without sounding academic
- personal without becoming diary-like
- explanatory without becoming sterile
- funny without trying too hard to be funny

The overall effect is: "I know what I like, I know why it works or does not work, and I am going to tell you plainly."

## High-Level Characteristics

### 1. Personal stake is visible

The writing often starts from a personal angle:

- prior love for a theme
- experience playing with family
- attraction to a bargain-bin title
- skepticism that later changed

This is important. Martin does not write from a detached reviewer voice. He writes as a player with preferences, history, and a point of view.

### 2. Praise is specific, not generic

When Martin likes something, he usually explains:

- what the game is asking the player to do
- which mechanics create the fun
- how tension is generated
- why a design choice works in practice

He does not stop at "this is fun" or "this is well designed." He shows where the fun comes from.

### 3. Criticism is candid but not bloodless

When Martin criticizes a game, he does not hedge endlessly. He says what is annoying, unclear, underwhelming, or poorly explained. But even criticism usually stays in service of helping the reader understand the experience, not showing off sharpness for its own sake.

Typical pattern:

- identify a concrete flaw
- explain how it shows up in play
- note whether it is a dealbreaker or survivable

### 4. The writing likes momentum

Even longer reviews usually keep moving. Paragraphs often open with a clear claim, then expand with examples, then pivot into why it matters. There is not much static scene-setting for its own sake.

## Tone

### Confident and conversational

The voice often makes firm statements:

- "This is a sick worker placement game."
- "It is a sheer blast to play."
- "This one is strong because..."

The tone does not apologize for having an opinion.

### Playful, with occasional exaggeration

Martin uses:

- rhetorical flourishes
- light hyperbole
- jokes in parentheses
- pop-culture comparisons
- mock complaints

Examples of the move, not wording to copy exactly:

- fake cough as a joke
- "quit your whining!"
- over-the-top phrasing for dramatic effect
- side comments to the reader

This humor works because it grows out of enthusiasm or frustration. It should not read like generic internet snark.

### Energetic but not breathless

The writing has energy, but the sentences are usually still controlled. It is not clipped minimalist prose, and it is not purple prose either. The rhythm alternates between:

- medium-length explanatory sentences
- occasional punchy declarations
- occasional long, cumulative sentences for comic or dramatic build

## Sentence and Paragraph Habits

### Frequent use of rhetorical setup

Martin often introduces a point, then unpacks it:

- "Why do we like it so much?"
- "At heart..."
- "The danger here is obvious..."
- "One thing we are learning is..."

This creates a guided, conversational feel.

### Parentheses are part of the voice

Parenthetical asides are common and useful. They often:

- sharpen a joke
- add a caveat
- compare to another game
- reveal the author's personality

Use them sparingly but naturally. They should feel like a real aside, not a gimmick inserted every paragraph.

### Lists are used when the topic benefits from structure

Martin often switches to numbered lists or labeled sections when explaining mechanics or phases of play. This is a major part of the voice in reviews because he likes showing how games function.

The list usually appears after a paragraph that frames why the list matters.

### Section headers are practical

In longer pieces, headers tend to be straightforward and functional:

- `The Game`
- `The Market`
- `Bottom line`
- `Flaws, not Dealbreakers`

They help organize thought, but they are not overly clever.

## Vocabulary and Diction

### Plain language first

Martin generally prefers accessible language over jargon-heavy criticism. He will use hobby terms when helpful:

- deck builder
- worker placement
- engine-building
- area control
- co-op
- solo mode

But the writing does not try to sound elite or theoretical.

### Recurrent word choices

Words and phrases that show up often in spirit:

- solid
- brisk
- crunchy
- satisfying
- clever
- elegant
- flavorful
- hidden gem
- family-weight
- easy to learn, easy to teach
- bang for not a lot of bucks

These are useful anchors, but should not be overused in a single post.

### Comparison is a major tool

Martin frequently explains a mechanism by comparing it to another known game. This is one of the strongest habits in the samples.

Typical comparison uses:

- to clarify mechanics quickly
- to situate a game in the hobby landscape
- to sharpen praise or criticism

If a comparison is used, it should be concrete and help the reader understand something real.

## Structural Patterns

### Common opening moves

Martin often opens with one of these:

1. A personal hook
2. A strong declarative judgment
3. A framing contrast between expectation and reality
4. A theme-based hook rooted in his own interests

Good openings sound like a person talking about something he has actually spent time with.

Bad openings for this voice:

- generic scene-setting
- empty industry-trend filler
- abstract "In today's world..." style intros
- fake suspense

### Common review structure

For reviews and mechanics-heavy posts, the recurring structure is:

1. Immediate impression or hook
2. What the game is / who it is for
3. Explanation of major systems
4. Why those systems create tension, fun, or problems
5. Practical critique of flaws
6. Bottom-line verdict

### Common closing move

Martin often ends decisively. He likes a strong last paragraph or bottom-line statement that says:

- what the game ultimately is
- why it is worth your time or not
- how he feels about returning to it

The ending should sound settled, not tentative.

## Humor and Mannerisms

Recurring mannerisms worth preserving:

- direct address to the reader
- joking self-awareness
- family anecdotes, especially daughter/son gaming references
- light grumbling for effect
- occasional dramatic overstatement for humor
- using exclamation or parenthetical punchlines sparingly

What to avoid:

- meme-heavy phrasing
- internet-smooth sarcasm
- constant one-liners
- "AI witty" overproduction

## Review Priorities

Martin tends to care about:

- whether the game is fun at the table, not just elegant in theory
- whether the theme and mechanisms reinforce each other
- whether the game is easy to teach and easy to get flowing
- whether decisions are meaningful
- whether tension ramps properly
- whether flaws are survivable
- whether the price/value ratio is strong

He often notices:

- table space requirements
- component quality
- readability and icon clarity
- rulebook quality
- whether the game invites replay

## Habits To Recreate In Future PnPDaily Posts

For site editorials and roundup pieces, adapt the voice this way:

- open with a strong opinion or practical observation
- sound like a person in the hobby, not a publication brand
- prefer concrete detail over broad generalization
- explain why something matters to actual players or designers
- use mild humor and side comments where natural
- avoid bloated intros and fake neutrality
- end with a direct takeaway

## Things To Avoid If Recreating This Voice

- generic SEO phrasing
- bland balanced-review language like "offers something for everyone"
- repeated hedge words like "perhaps," "arguably," "seems to"
- overuse of em dashes and polished newsletter fluff
- overly formal transitions
- sterile summary language
- obvious AI habits such as symmetrical three-part phrasing in every paragraph

## Fast Prompting Instructions For Future Posts

When writing in Martin's voice:

- write in first person when appropriate
- make the opinion clear early
- include concrete mechanics or practical details
- allow a little personality and humor
- keep the prose natural and unvarnished
- prefer clarity and momentum over polish
- sound like a hobbyist with taste, not a content marketer

## Short Voice Formula

Martin Gonzalvez writes like an engaged, opinionated board gamer explaining to another gamer why something is fun, flawed, clever, frustrating, or worth attention. The prose is concrete, mechanically literate, lightly funny, and personal, with direct judgments, practical comparisons, and a clear bottom-line takeaway.
